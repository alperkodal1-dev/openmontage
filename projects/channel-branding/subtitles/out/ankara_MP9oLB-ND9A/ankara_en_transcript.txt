July 28, 1402.
On the plain of Ankara, two great Turkish armies stood face to face.
One sultan would end the day in chains, betrayed by his own men.
But why would they turn on him?
Two Turkish rulers, two empires, and a world too small for both of them.
In the East, Timur, the lame conqueror who left towers of skulls behind him.
In the West, Bayezid, the Thunderbolt, the sultan who crushed the Crusaders at Nicopolis.
In the year 1400, two fugitive kings fled from Timur's armies
and found shelter at the Ottoman court.
They were Sultan Ahmed Jalayir, the ruler of Baghdad,
and Kara Yusuf, the chief of the Black Sheep Turkomans.
And so it began with letters.
Timur wrote: Hand over the princes who fled to you.
And remember who you are, the son of a boatman.
Bayezid answered: I do not betray those who seek my protection.
Come, lame one.
And if you do not come, may your wives be divorced from you three times.
In the Islamic world, a threefold divorce could never be undone.
To wish it upon another man was to call him less than a man,
unable to keep even his own household.
It was among the gravest insults a ruler could put in writing,
and Timur would never forgive it.
Those words could not be taken back.
That summer, Timur marched into Anatolia.
He took Sivas, and Bayezid's son died there.
But Timur did not stay. He turned south to burn Aleppo,
and Bayezid believed the storm had passed
and returned to the walls of Constantinople. He was wrong.
In the spring of 1402, Timur came back.
Bayezid lifted the siege and marched east through the summer heat,
but Timur slipped around him and was waiting at Ankara.
There, under the July sun, Timur cut off the river.
North of the battlefield, near the town of Çubuk,
Timur's men dammed the stream and turned its water into a reservoir behind their own lines.
Downstream, where the Ottomans would make their camp, the riverbed ran dry,
and the Ottoman army fought thirsty.
Bayezid's Serbian knights proved their loyalty to the Thunderbolt,
and no one resisted Timur harder than they did. His janissaries held the center.
Then, in the heart of the battle, the Tatar horsemen turned and rode to Timur.
Why? Timur had sent them secret word before the battle.
We are of one blood, of one steppe. Why die for a sultan who keeps you as subjects?
And Bayezid, it was said, had kept his treasury locked while his soldiers went unpaid.
The Anatolian soldiers, seeing their old lords at Timur's side, followed them.
Bayezid fought on, on a hill with his last loyal men, until the sun went down.
By nightfall, the Thunderbolt was a prisoner.
Some chronicles whisper that Timur carried him from city to city, locked in an iron cage.
Truth or legend?
No one knows for certain. Eight months later, he was dead,
and the Ottoman Empire nearly died with him.
His sons fought each other for 11 years.
Timur died in 1405 on his way to China, but the Ottomans survived.
And 51 years later, Bayezid's great-grandson took Constantinople.
